# I. METHODOLOGY

## Selection of Armor Actions for Study

The criteria for actions to be studied were those set by Systems Analysis, Department of Defense (SA/DOD), to meet its research needs. They were as follows:

1. The actions were to be primarily tank-against-tank. The client expressed strong interest, however, in the effect of supporting arms upon such armor battles.

2. If possible, large numbers of tanks should be involved, preferably an armored division or more on each side.

3. Terrain must be relatively open; no actions in jungle or excessively mountainous terrain should be considered.

About halfway through the study, in further discussions with representatives of SA/DOD, the following additional desirable criterion was set forth:

4. If possible, at least one action treated should be a battle in which one side was defending prepared positions against a determined major attack.*

*SA/DOD also expressed interest in wearout and mechanical breakdown during or at the end of periods of breakout and pursuit. Such data could best be obtained, it was agreed, by following one or more armored divisions day by day through a long period including periods of both combat and noncombat.

## Assembling the Material

The data from US records on the tank actions selected was collected by those senior study participants who had been given responsibility for preparing papers on the actions (Andrews, Crosby, Nihart) with the help of research assistants. The research assistants, with the help of the Federal Records Center staff, searched the records available at the Center for the units involved, for the time periods of the actions. After action reports, division histories, unit diaries, journals, and periodic reports (G-2, G-3, G-4) were the basic sources for strength and loss figures. The contemporary accounts of the actions were also useful, as were comments on the effectiveness of US and enemy tanks.

German records in this country are discussed in Appendix C, attached. The German records were searched by a German-fluent senior staff member of HERO, assisted by a German-fluent research assistant.

## France-Flanders, May-June 1940

The Germans invaded the low countries and France in May 1940 and quickly won a stunning victory, despite substantial numerical inferiority (3,609 French and British tanks to 2,574 German tanks). The German tanks were also generally inferior in armor and firepower, although they were somewhat superior in mobility--and mobility in all its aspects was the key factor in this campaign.

The well-conceived German strategic plan called for a thrust down through the low countries which would draw the best French and British forces, whereupon the main German attack, 44 divisions strong and using most of the available German armor, would push through the Ardennes area and break through to the Channel, cutting off the Allied forces to the north. The plan worked smoothly. The French had thought the Ardennes impassable for armor, and had defended it so lightly that its roads were entirely adequate for the German tanks. The Meuse crossings were forced and the breakthrough accomplished with the support of strafing Stuka aircraft, whose devastating psychological effect substituted for the artillery which the German armored units had outrun.

### German Breakthrough and Exploitation North of the Somme, 10-24 May 1940

In this classic campaign the Germans concentrated 2,683 tanks, which qualitatively were somewhat inferior in gunpower and

German losses were 1,080, an estimated two-thirds of which were repairable, to Allied losses of 2,060 which in addition to combat losses, included total losses due to capture or destruction to avoid capture. Determining factors were the German exploitation of concentration at the decisive point; tactical, operational, and technical surprise; and mobility in all of its forms, physical, mental, organizational, doctrinal, command-control-communications, and the use of firepower and terrain. Conversely, the Allies, although possessing an overall greater number of tanks in France (3,609 Allied to 2,574 German), dispersed their tanks in France, in the area of the campaign, and organizationally. Allied tactics, doctrine, command-control-communications, and mental preparation was also deficient for this new form of warfare. This 2-to-1 disparity of tank losses, while in favor of Germany and significant, does not tell the complete story, as this campaign destroyed France as a military power and drove Britain from the continent.

### A Group Franc South of the Somme, June 1940

This small-unit action had a well-led and integrated French combat team launching a mobile counterattack from a defensive base and inflicting disproportionate losses on a German unit that had three times as many tanks but lacked reconnaissance or support. The French Group Franc consisted of 5 Somua tanks heavily armored for the time and armed with a very powerful 47mm. gun, 5 Panhard armored cars armed with 25mm. guns, 2 47mm. antitank guns, 2 25mm. antitank guns, heavy machineguns, and a motorcycle reconnaissance company. The Germans had 14 Pz.Kpfw. IV tanks with 30mm. of armor and a short 75mm. gun. The German tanks were the most powerful they possessed at the time, yet the French 47mm. guns easily penetrated them, while the German 75mm. gun of that time was only rarely effective against the 40mm. armor of the Somua. ## GOODWOOD, 18-22 July 1944

Operation GOODWOOD was a part of the Allied effort to break out of the Normandy beachhead in the vicinity of Caen. It is remarkable both for the effectiveness of preliminary aerial bombardment and for the misuse of tanks. British losses were very heavy (459 tanks lost or 36% of all engaged). German losses for this operation have not been found in the microfilmed records in the United States. The battle began with an extensive aerial bombardment which limited the initial response of the Germans to the British advance. However, as the British pushed further into the German defensive lines several weaknesses in their operational planning became apparent, while German defensive tactics were excellent. German armor and antitank guns were well placed to hit the advancing British tanks, which were trying to force the breakthrough without the aid of infantry. The British armor advanced beyond the range of supporting artillery fire; and the narrow attack zone bounded by minefields limited the number of tanks that could be committed and caused a severe traffic jam. GOODWOOD drew German armor off the US front and destroyed some of it; no breakthrough was achieved, but a continuation was made to the success of the later US breakthrough. British tank superiority (at least 4-1) and effective air bombardment resulted in a limited British advance.

## Arracourt, 19-22 September 1944

This tank battle near Arracourt, France, was won by Combat Command A of the US 4th Armored Division through superior leadership and tactics, despite a German numerical superiority of 5:3 (in the medium tanks that actually fought the battle), and despite the overall qualitative superiority of the German Mark IV (Pz.Kpfw. IV) and Mark V (Pz.Kpfw. V) tanks to the US M4. Responding to a German counterattack by the 111th and 113th Panzer Brigades, the US forces achieved a US-German loss ratio of 4:1 (87 German tanks lost to 21 US tanks lost). US leadership effectively used the principles of mass and maneuver, concentrating [overtyped: their] tanks and launching repeated spoiling attacks, while the Germans wasted their tanks in piecemeal commitments.

The superior mobility of the US M4 on dry, firm ground, and its power-traverse, quickly turning turret, more than compensated for the greater penetration capability of the German tank armament--75mm. guns, 43, 48, and 70 calibers long. Low visibility due to fog prevented US air support that would otherwise have been available, but the fog also allowed the Americans to close to a distance at which their less powerful L/40 guns became effective. Well-coordinated use of the highly effective tank destroyers and of artillery support contributed to the success. US use of white phosphorus as a first round allowed quick adjustment of fire, forced

## The Saar Campaign, 8 November-7 December 1944

Bad weather was the most influential factor in the Saar Campaign, November-December 1944. Continual rain made the ground nearly impassable and poor visibility prevented air support. The aim of the US Third Army offensive was to clear the southern part of the Saar Valley and eventually to establish a bridgehead over the Rhine. In this operation, the US 4th Armored Division (271 tanks) was opposed by 9 German divisions, 2 brigades, and some miscellaneous units (145 tanks). The German Mark IVs, Mark Vs, and Mark VIs were superior to the US M4s in flotation, gunpower, and armor protectin. Despite their smaller force, the Germans fought a successful delaying action. They suited their tactics to take full advantage of the geographic and climatic conditions, while--because of these circumstances--the United States was engaged in an offensive for which tanks with marginal flotation were not well suited. Including 10 tanks that were recovered and returned to service, the US losses were 56 tanks to the German loss of 61. Thus although the Germans were outnumbered in tank strength almost 2:1, tank losses were almost equal for the two sides. Ten US M5s and 45 miscellaneous German tanks that were present were not suitable for action against opposing armor and antitank weapons, and if they

## St. Vith, 17-23 December 1944

The mission of CCB of the 7th Armored Division at St. Vith was to hold at all costs in order to disrupt the German Ardennes offensive. Through use of excellent defensive tactics it did hold for six days against greatly superior German forces, delaying the Germans long enough to make certain the failure of the German offensive. Inadequate German planning allowed the German offensive spearheads to be held up by traffic jam, so that they reached St. Vith after the 7th Armored. The Germans committed their tanks piecemeal for three days, wasting the advantage of their numbers. CCB used a mobile defense, defending dominant terrain with dug-in infantry and tank destroyers, while holding its tanks as a mobile counterattacking force. Repeated US spoiling attacks kept the Germans off balance and made them believe they faced a much larger force than they did. The mobility of the M4 tank permitted the successful implementation of these tactics. /Loss ratios to be included when charts are completed./

## Bastogne, 17-20 December 1944

In the area east of Bastogne, in southeastern Belgium, on the edge of the Ardennes Forest, elements of two American armored divisions, the 9th and the 10th, clashed with elements of three

The German XLVII Panzer Corps, to which the German divisions belonged, was driving westward around Bastogne toward the Meuse River, as part of their great Ardennes counteroffensive. The Americans were assigned the task of holding them back from the roads leading into the city. CCR of the 9th Armored Division and CCB of the 10th were posted at various points in task forces comprising approximately a battalion apiece, variously supported by artillery, armored infantry, and other elements. Most of the action took place in fog, smoke, or darkness, with the Germans attacking with small numbers of tanks, and generally at fairly close range since poor visibility prevented their taking advantage of their longer range guns. Tanks, tank destroyers, artillery, and infantry worked together in teams to drive off repeated attacks or, to exact a high price for their own destruction. The Germans, surprised to find their way blocked, lacked the information and the leadership to coordinate their attacks and to remove quickly the obstacles which they certainly had the strength to overcome. They did ultimately get through, but not until their schedule had been disrupted and their attack thrown off balance.

## Alam Halfa, 31 August-3 September 1942

Elements of the British 7th and 10th Armored Divisions conducted a successful defense of Alam Halfa in the Egyptian Western Desert against General Erwin Rommel's Afrika Corps (15th and 21st Panzer Divisions) by using their tanks dug in, in a static defensive role. This unconventional use of armor was dictated by the qualitative inferiority of the Grant medium tanks with which the British were equipped. The Grants had 75mm. short guns (31 cal. long) with relatively low muzzle velocity, and speed not significantly higher than that of the opposing Pz.Kpfw. IIIs and IVs, which were much more heavily gunned (50mm., L/60 and 75mm., L/43 or L/48). The usefulness of the Grant gun was further severely limited by its low side mount, which permitted little adjustment of elevation or traverse. The Grants' high silhouettes also made them easy targets on the battlefield and, as the British used them, the tank commander had to partly expose himself in order to direct fire.

A breakthrough at Alam Halfa was essential to Rommel, as he could no longer support himself in Egypt unless he could break through to Alexandria and Cairo. Thus, although the numerically superior British force lost tanks in a ratio of 1.34:1, Rommel's repulse was not only a British tactical success, but also a significant strategic victory.

## Chouigui Pass--26 November 1942

Tank speed and maneuverability, together with a near-perfect tactical plan, were the main factors influencing the small-unit US victory at Chouigui Pass in Tunisia, the first US armor action of World War II. In overall qualitative characteristics, the German tanks outclassed the American tanks. A detachment of the German 190th Panzer Battalion, 10th Panzer Division, was fighting with 13 Mark III and Mark IV medium tanks, all of which were equipped with 50mm. and 75mm. guns. The US force--two companies of the 1st Battalion, 1st Armored Regiment, had 25 tanks, all light M3s, with 37mm. guns, 53 calibers long that were completely incapable of penetrating the German armor. The mobility of the light tanks, coupled with superior tactics, enabled the Americans to hit the German tanks in the rear, engines, and tracks, resulting in a US loss of only 28% of the tanks engaged, and a German loss of 69%. The US to German loss ratio was 1:1.29.

## Targul Frumos, May 1944

A German Panzer Division under General Hasso von Manteuffel and a Soviet tank army (or motorized army) opposed each other on a broad open plain in the Battle of Targul Frumos. Speed and maneuverability were the important factors and in these the German Mark IVs and Mark VIs surpassed the powerfully gunned Soviet JS-III. The Russians were able to surprise the Germans by opening fire with their high-velocity 120mm. gun at a range which was far beyond the effective range of the German tank guns. When the Germans were within their normal firing range their guns were unable to penetrate the heavy Soviet armor. The Germans closed the distance quickly and, due to superior mobility, maneuvered around the Russian tanks to hit their thinner side and rear armor. The result was a clear-cut German victory, achieved by superior leadership and tactical ability and facilitated by certain weaknesses in the JS-III--a slow hand traverse mechanism for a very heavy turret, a meager supply of ammunition because the rounds were so large that fewer rounds filled up more space, and heavy separate hand-loading ammunition that slowed the rate of fire. Of a total of 410 tanks the Russians lost 350 and 60 others were damaged; the Germans lost only 11 tanks out of a total of 160.

# III. ANALYSIS

A study of the armored force engagements contained in this study suggest that there are three major kinds of tank operational characteristics, and two important subsidiary characteristics, which affect the outcome of armored engagements. These five characteristics, listed in what appears to be the order of their relative importance, are as follows:

1. Tactical Mobility (Substantial speed combined with cross-country capability under any likely combat circumstances, to include if possible a slight margin of superiority over enemy tanks of comparable role and characteristics.)

2. Firepower (Ability to fire quickly, accurately, and repeatedly, with a weapon whose projectile has sufficient mass and muzzle velocity to destroy a likely opposing tank at reasonable range.)

3. Protection (Full protection against small arms, shell fragments, and long-range fire from the most likely possible armored opponent.)

4. Endurance (Ability to operate effectively over substantial periods of time and extensive distances; this is also an aspect of strategic mobility, and is enhanced by well-organized logistical support.)

1

<!-- page 29 -->

OFFICIAL USE ONLY

5. Reliability (Minimum of breakdowns under the combat circumstances to be expected; ease of first echelon maintenance.)

Also emerging from the experiences in this study there appear to be five major prerequisites for the effective employment of armored forces:

1. Aggressive, imaginative, skilled leadership, to exploit one's own advantages and minimize one's own weaknesses with respect to the opponent.

2. A coherent and effective doctrine for the employment of armor.

3. Effective training in the established doctrine to assure common understanding and maximum coordination capability.

4. Maximum possible combined arms coordination with available artillery, infantry, and air support, in accordance with the established doctrine.

5. Employment of tanks in mass, to the maximum extent possible under the circumstances.

From these experiences it is possible to make several general observations:

1. Numerically fewer tanks, with superior operational characteristics, are particularly effective in the defense, although this effectiveness will be seriously reduced if they do not have firepower superiority over the enemy.

2. Numbers, in general, are more important than firepower or protection, because of the advantages superior numbers provide in maneuver and mass. Number, however, are evidently less important than mobility, unless the numerical inferiority is overwhelming.

3. In engagements of large numbers of adequate tanks, the side with the greatest mobility is usually victorious, unless it is greatly outnumbered, or unless its tanks are seriously inferior in one of the three major operational characteristics. The turning point in terms of the relationship of mobility to numbers depends on too many factors to be specified precisely, but the effects of disparity of numbers are obviously reduced by effective doctrine, adequate training, and good leadership on the side with numerical inferiority.

These supporting weapons and units have the primary effect of enhancing the mobility of friendly tanks, and inhibiting the mobility of the opposition.

The study also suggests certain conclusions with respect to various mixes of leadership, numbers, and weapons. If all the variables and intangible factors of combat (leadership, terrain, morale, availability of supporting troops, etc.) are equal, and if the size of the forces is approximately equal, tanks with superior characteristics will ordinarily defeat an equal number of tanks with lesser characteristics. If the tanks of one side have a superiority in one or more characteristics, while those of the other side have superiority in other characteristics, the outcome of a conflict between equal numbers is much harder to predict. The results of engagements studied would suggest, however, that if the tanks on both sides meet at least minimum operational requirements for adequacy in all five operational characteristics, then the listed order of priority of the five characteristics (mobility, firepower, protection, endurance, and reliability) become particularly significant. There should be exploration of the possibility of assigning relative quantified values to these characteristics, for comparative purposes.

It is understood that the purpose of this study is to assist policymakers in deciding an allocation of available resources to obtain the maximum return for armored conflict. Thus, it is essential at the outset to determine how and why tanks can contribute to success in battle. It is undeniable that the best antitank weapon is a tank--but it is an extremely expensive weapon. The resources of men, complicated manufactured materials, and money required for production, training, and deployment could produce a number of high-velocity antitank guns, tank mines, and the highly mobile troops required to man these guns and place these mines, which would in sum be more effective in antitank defense than would the single tank they replace. Thus, if our mission is simply to defeat other tanks, the best allocation of resources might well be to antitank means other than tanks.

It is clear, however, that an enemy tank force meeting such a defense of antitank guns and mines would employ a combined arms approach in which infantry, artillery, and engineers, supported by tanks, would be employed.

To counter such a combined arms offensive against our antitank defenses, it would be essential to have, in addition to the purely antitank defense, adequate quantities of defending infantry and artillery, plus, probably, a small number of very powerful tanks, particularly strong in firepower and protection to work with infantry and artillery in opposing hostile attacks and to

At the same time, even if the defense should be completely successful, there is some question whether the smaller number of very powerful tanks would be able to exploit such a defensive success as well as a larger number of somewhat less expensive tanks.

While tanks are highly effective as antitank weapons, it is clear from our studies that they are also especially effective, both as weapons and as components of a combined-arms team, in employing their mobility and shock action capability in offensive action against both unarmored and armored hostile forces. A successful defense is at least partially wasted if the defenders are not thereupon able to seize the initiative in offensive action. It is clear from the examples we have studied that a smaller number of very powerful tanks, particularly if not more mobile than the opponent's, is not likely to be so successful in an attack against enemy armor as would be a larger number. It would thus appear that resources available for armor along should be so allocated as to produce maximum numbers of tanks that are adequate in the four lower priority characteristics, and that are highly mobile under all likely combat circumstances. It would appear to be a serious mistake to try to approach the optimum in all five characteristics, if this were to mean a substantially smaller number of tanks.

But it should be further borne in mind that the primary role of the tank is offensive, rather than defensive, and that other, nonarmored measures are available to offset tank deficiencies in numbers and quality in the defense.

On balance, then, the effectiveness of tanks in their primary missions of maneuver and shock action is at least directly proportional to the number of "adequate" tanks available. This would seem to justify a production policy which will assure the maximum number of tanks which at least meet all five of the minimum operational requirements, and which exceed the minimum mobility requirement.

# IV. EVALUATION OF ANALYTICAL FINDINGS

## Limitations on Data and on Analysis

For reasons discussed earlier in this report (Section I, Methodology), it has been necessary to proceed as rapidly as possible to the analysis of as many individual tank engagements for which sufficient data was available to permit a reasonably valid analysis of armored conflict. While every possible effort was made to find actions representative of as wide a variety of tank engagements as possible, the initial approach was necessarily in the nature of a net-casting process, rather than a deliberate approach to analysis of the results of specific actions, or series of actions, as part of a planned and systematic procedure. Furthermore, lack of available data for some actions or campaigns which had been on the original list has led still further toward a collection of individual studies selected for convenience, rather than as elements of a sound, systems approach.

The engagements studied are for the most part those for which primary and secondary source material is readily available because the actions were in themselves dramatic or unusual, or were parts of larger, dramatic operations. Not unexpectedly, these are more often than not actions in which a high order of leadership, or special circumstances, resulted in victory by tank

units that were outnumbered, or were outgunned, or that otherwise suffered under some serious disadvantage. Fortunately, there are enough of these actions, in different armies, to provide some sort of balance, but this balance is largely fortuitous. It is not clear from our data whether the actions we describe were typical, or quite atypical, or something in between. We suspect the latter, but we cannot be certain from the data that we have.

This does not in any way invalidate the data, analysis, or the findings, of any of the individual actions investigated in this study, nor--we believe--does it invalidate the overall conclusions we shall draw from review of these findings in the next section of this report. But it does mean that we cannot be certain that we have all of the conclusions which might be desirable for the purposes of this study.

What is particularly lacking from this study is an indication of the effect of sustained operations on armored conflict, and on different kinds of tanks with varying characteristics, particularly in the kind of war of rapid and long-range movement for which armor is particularly suited. In the time available for this study there was not sufficient time nor funds to undertake the painstaking collection of basic primary source data on the operations of one or more units over extended periods of time, to include all of the opposing force data essential to the analysis.
